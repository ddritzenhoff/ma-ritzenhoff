\chapter{Background}
\label{chap:background}
This chapter lays the groundwork for our investigations by covering several key areas. We first provide foundational information on the QUIC protocol by comparing it to \ac{TCP}. Subsequently, we cover \ac{eBPF}, \ac{XDP}, and AF\_XDP, followed by an introduction to \ac{PTP}, and finally, an overview of the \ac{QIR} framework.

\section{A TCP and QUIC Comparison}
\label{sec:the-quic-protocol}
QUIC~\cite{rfc9000} is a next-generation transport protocol developed to address the inherent limitations of protocols like \ac{TCP} and \ac{TLS}. It offers several key advantages: a combined transport and cryptographic handshake for faster connection establishment, native stream multiplexing to prevent head-of-line blocking and improved throughput resilience to packet loss, robust connection migration for seamless transitions between networks, and easier deployment.

Google, the original developer of QUIC, was already serving 50\% of all requests to Google services from Chrome browsers over QUIC by 2015~\cite{chromium_quic_2015}. Today, \ac{HTTP/3}, which uses QUIC, is enabled by default on Chrome browsers~\cite{chromium_quicer_2024}. These statistics underscore the protocol's clear benefits and its widespread adoption across the Internet. Figure~\ref{fig:network-stacks} illustrates the respective technology stacks of \ac{HTTP/3} and its predecessor, HTTP/2. The following paragraphs dive deeper into implications of such changes.

\subsection{Head-of-Line Blocking}
\label{subsec:hol-blocking}
QUIC was specifically designed to overcome several of \ac{TCP}'s inherent shortcomings, most notably \ac{HoL} blocking. Similar to QUIC, \ac{TCP}'s design mandates the in-order delivery of packets within a single connection. However, the problem in \ac{TCP} arises when even a single packet is delayed or dropped en-route. For higher-level protocols like HTTP/2 that build off \ac{TCP}, this means all subsequent packets are forced to wait for the retransmission of the lost packet, irrespective of the application-level stream they belong to. Figure~\ref{fig:hol-blocking} illustrates this phenomenon. Consequently, this property makes HTTP/2 highly vulnerable to performance degradation from packet loss, especially when utilizing multiple streams. With QUIC, the concept of streams is native to the protocol itself, which lets \ac{HTTP/3} effectively multiplex without any risk of \ac{HoL} blocking.

Head-of-Line blocking ensures that the HTTP/2 client only receives packets 1, 2, and 3. Packet 4 must be resent for the HTTP/2 client to receive any subsequent packets. The QUIC client will receive all packets but packet 4. The HTTP/3 client will receive all packets but 4, 5, and 11, which are part of the one stream that is blocked.}

\end{figure}

\subsection{Iteration Velocity}
\label{subsec:slow-adoption-of-new-features}
Integrating \ac{TCP}'s codebase directly within the operating system kernel, while beneficial for avoiding costly packet copying to userspace, introduces substantial drawbacks. This approach leads to a comparatively slow adoption rate for new features or bug fixes, and significantly raises the stakes for every code change. For any new \ac{TCP} enhancement to achieve widespread use, it requires all clients to upgrade their kernel versions to the one where the feature was introduced. This tight coupling creates brittleness for applications dependent on particular \ac{TCP} features, as they may not function correctly if paired with an outdated kernel. This contrasts sharply with protocols like QUIC, which operates in userspace and is integrated directly into the application itself. Consequently, updating QUIC is as simple as bundling the newest version of the QUIC library with the application code, thereby eliminating this brittleness entirely. Furthermore, userspace offers a more forgiving environment for experimentation. If a new algorithm or feature contains a bug, it is far less likely to crash the entire operating system than with \ac{TCP}, making development and testing safer and quicker.

\subsection{Transport and Cryptographic Handshakes}
\label{subsec:combined-transport-and-cryptographic-handshakes}
With \ac{TCP}+\ac{TLS}, a round trip is needed to establish the \ac{TCP} connection, and another round trip is then required to make it secure. QUIC, however, integrates \ac{TLS} directly into its protocol, combining these two steps into a single handshake, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:quic-tcp-handshakes}. This integration typically leads to a reduced time-to-first-byte. Additionally, in the special case that QUIC has previously communicated with a server, it can leverage cached cryptographic parameters and connection settings to transmit application data during the very first round-trip~\cite{rfc9000}. This is known as \ac{0-RTT}.

\subsection{Security, Privacy, and Ossification}
\label{subsec:security-and-privacy}
Another downside to \ac{TLS} and \ac{TCP} is that they leave header information exposed to middleboxes. In fact, none of \ac{TCP}'s header components is encrypted. This exposure contributes to a larger attack surface area for malicious actors but also contributes to network ossification. Middleboxes can enforce outdated \ac{TCP} behaviors and hinder protocol evolution by overwriting visible values or by expecting specific field contents and dropping packets that don't adhear to them. In contrast, QUIC encrypts a substantial portion of its header fields, including the packet number, key phase, and reserved bits, making it far less susceptible to such issues. Even fields not fully encrypted, like the Connection ID, are integrity-protected. This design allows for immediate detection of packet tampering and severely impedes a \ac{MITM} attacker from altering values. While \ac{TCP} and \ac{TLS} technically also provide integrity protection by means of the checksum field, the checksum is a simple 1's complement sum, which is feasible for an attacker to recompute.

To further combat ossification, QUIC also employs a technique known as greasing. One instance of such usage is with the 'QUIC Bit', which is the second most significant bit of the first byte in every QUIC packet. While this bit is typically fixed and is used to aid in protocol identification, QUIC endpoints may negotiate using the \texttt{grease\_quic\_bit} transport parameter to send an arbitrary value instead~\cite{rfc9287}. This consequently encourages middleboxes to tolerate unknown or future extensions rather than relying on a static value.

\subsection{Connection Migration}
\label{subsec:connection-migration}
Unlike \ac{TCP}, which binds connections to the source and destination IP addresses and ports, QUIC maintains connections with a \ac{CID}. The advantage is that it allows QUIC to migrate across underlying network changes, such as from Wi-Fi to cellular data, without needing to re-establish the entire connection, as is the case for \ac{TCP}. Even when the source IP changes, the client may continue using the same \ac{CID}. This is especially useful for mobile environments given the almost ubiquitous support of both Wi-Fi and cellular.

\subsection{Packet Ambiguity}
\label{subsec:packet-ambiguity}
When a packet goes missing in \ac{TCP}, a retransmission from the server to the client carries the same sequence number and data as the original. This introduces ambiguity, making it difficult to discern if an incoming packet is a delayed original or a new copy. QUIC resolves this ambiguity through the use of monotonically growing packet numbers. If a packet is lost, a new packet will be generated with a unique, incremented packet number, potentially containing retransmitted payload. An additional benefit of this approach is the enhanced visibility of packet loss, which in turn enables more fine-grained tuning of QUIC's congestion control algorithms.

\subsection{QUIC's Limitations}
\label{subsec:quic-limitations}
Despite its many benefits, QUIC faces existing drawbacks that hinder its performance, especially within the context of high-bandwidth networks. As demonstrated by Jaeger et al.~\cite{10186365}, \ac{TCP} and \ac{TLS} achieved a throughput of 8010 Mbit/s on a 10 Gbit/s link, whereas the top-performing QUIC implementation measured only 3004 Mbit/s. As previously discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction}, this performance disparity is largely attributable to inefficient packet I/O, which can be optimized. However, other significant challenges exist.

Consider a scenario where a server, acting like a streaming service, sends static data stored in non-volatile memory to a client. A TCP-based solution leveraging \ac{kTLS} and the \texttt{sendfile(2)} system call (as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:memory-bandwidth-TCP}) can efficiently transfer data with only a single copy into kernel memory from storage. In contrast, because QUIC operates as a userspace application, this process involves an additional memory copy: first from storage into kernel memory, and then from kernel memory into the application's userspace memory. As depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:memory-bandwidth-QUIC}, this incurs not only increased CPU cycles for the copying but also consumes significant memory bandwidth. While introducing a new API could potentially eliminate this extra copy, such a change would carry serious security implications by blurring the critical distinction between kernel and userspace capabilities.

This all goes to say that QUIC itself is not a silver bullet, and there exist many areas for improvement.

\section{eBPF}
\label{sec:bpf}
\ac{eBPF}, also simply known as \ac{BPF}, is a Linux-native in-kernel bytecode language and virtual machine that allows for programmable, event-driven execution across many of the kernel's subsystems. It represents an evolution from \ac{cBPF}, which also offers an in-kernel bytecode language and virtual machine but with fewer capabilities. The work flow for creating an \ac{eBPF} program typically entails writing the program in a subset of C, compiling it using a tool like LLVM, writing the userspace loader program, and finally loading and attaching the program. Once a specific event occurs, the \ac{eBPF} program will subsequently be invoked. Finally, because it is a Linux-native technology, \ac{BPF} comes packaged with every kernel version since 3.18~\cite{starovoitov2014bpf}.

\subsection{Instruction Set}
\label{subsec:instruction-set}
\ac{BPF} is a general purpose \ac{RISC} architecture which can be used as a compilation target for
compiler back ends. Typically written in a subset of C that does not allow features such as arbitrary loops, unbounded recursion, and arbitrary memory accesses, \ac{BPF} bytecode is loaded by the kernel at runtime and is later \ac{JIT} compiled into native opcodes for better performance. This approach allows the kernel to become programmable without having to cross the user-kernel space boundary. This is incredibly advantageous because it offers a flexible new way of gaining visibility into the Linux kernel as well as boosting data path performance by enabling data handling at the earliest possible stages. Moreover, BPF programs can be atomically updated at runtime without requiring a kernel restart, allowing for dynamic changes throughout a program's lifecycle.

\ac{BPF} consists of eleven 64 bit registers with 32 bit subregisters. It also contains a 512 byte stack space and a program counter. Each of the eleven registers are named \texttt{r0 - r10}, and the default operating mode is 64 bits. The 32 bit subregisters are only accessible through ALU operations. Table~\ref{tab:ebpf_registers} shows the BPF calling convention.

Because of the simple nature of \ac{BPF}'s calling convention, it can map directly to \texttt{x86\_64} and \texttt{arm64}, which greatly benefits the \ac{JIT} compiler given that it needs only to issue a single call instruction when placing function arguments.

\subsection{Helper Functions}
\label{subsec:helper-functions}
Depending on the hook point, a given \ac{eBPF} program will have access to a number of helper functions, which provide assistance in retrieving and pushing data to the kernel. Using the registers defined in Table~\ref{tab:ebpf_registers}, a typical helper function signature will take a form similar to \texttt{u64 fn(u64 r1, u64 r2, u64 r3, u64 r4, u64 r5)}. To ensure a stable and architecture-agnostic interface, the kernel then abstracts helper functions using the macros \texttt{BPF\_CALL\_0} to \texttt{BPF\_CALL\_5}. These macros precisely define how arguments are passed from the eBPF virtual machine's registers to the kernel helper function, allowing the JIT compiler to issue direct call instructions across various CPU architectures.

\subsection{Maps}
\label{subsec:ebpf-maps}
\ac{eBPF} maps represent the way information is passed between the \ac{eBPF} program and userspace application. They are key-value stores that live inside the kernel and are the data structure typically used for managing state within \ac{eBPF} programs. They also allow data to persist across multiple program executions. As seen within Figure~\ref{fig:bpf-map}, processes in userspace access maps through file descriptors, and a map may also be shared between multiple processes and \ac{BPF} programs.

\subsection{Limitations}
While powerful, \ac{eBPF} isn't a silver bullet. Its capabilities remain significantly more constrained than those of a typical userspace application. For example, it doesn't support function calls with six or more arguments, the strict verifier might reject a program if it can't definitively prove termination, and \ac{eBPF} programs have access to only a very limited set of helper functions and a small stack size. Finally, debugging is basic. It is currently not possible to step through an \ac{eBPF} program while it runs in the kernel, which consequently makes print debugging the most effective method. For most use cases, userspace applications are still the most viable option.

\section{XDP}
\label{sec:xdp}

\ac{XDP} is a hook point situated very early in the network receive path, and it allows a loaded \ac{BPF} program to be invoked for every packet. Seen in Figure~\ref{fig:xdp-receive-path}, there are two \ac{XDP} hook points: \texttt{XDP\_DRV} and \texttt{XDP\_SKB}. The former represents the earliest point at which a packet may be processed in software, but it comes with the cost of requiring integration into the network driver. When this is not an option, \texttt{XDP\_SKB} offers a generic hook point which functions for every setup, but requires additional per-packet processing.

One stipulation to loading an \ac{XDP} program is that it may only be attached to a single queue on a specific \ac{NIC}. This is an important consideration as packet steering mechanisms like \ac{RSS} are often used to distribute different flows across the queues of a particular \ac{NIC}. Therefore, to capture all relevant traffic, it is crucial to either attach the program to every single queue or to combine the queues together using a command like \texttt{sudo ethtool -L <interface\_name> combined <number\_of\_queues>}. This directs the \ac{NIC} to use a specified number of combined receive and transmit queues.

\end{figure}

\subsection{Metadata}
Depending on whether \texttt{XDP\_DRV} or \texttt{XDP\_SKB} is used, access to different sources of information is available, presenting a trade-off between early access to raw data and later access to richer, more context-aware information.

\subsubsection{xdp\_md}
\label{subsec:xdp-md}
Upon entry of every single \texttt{XDP\_DRV} program, a struct \texttt{xdp\_md} is passed as an argument, providing essential information about the packet. Listing~\ref{lst:xdp-md} shows the struct's definition, which contains pointers to the beginning and end of the data as well as the index of the network interface on which the packet was received, the receive queue that handled the packet, and a metadata field where network drivers or \ac{XDP} programs can store custom information associated with the packet. As seen in Figure~\ref{fig:xdp-receive-path}, an \texttt{sk\_buff} has not yet been allocated, which is the reason for having less metadata available.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={The xdp\_md struct}, label={lst:xdp-md}]
struct xdp_md {
	__u32 data;
	__u32 data_end;
	__u32 data_meta;
	/* Below access go through struct xdp_rxq_info */
	__u32 ingress_ifindex; /* rxq->dev->ifindex */
	__u32 rx_queue_index;  /* rxq->queue_index  */
	__u32 egress_ifindex;  /* txq->dev->ifindex */
};
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{sk\_buff}
\label{subsec:sk-buff}
The \texttt{sk\_buff} struct, which stands for socket buffer, and whose definition can be seen in Listing~\ref{lst:sk-buff}, is the central data structure representing a network packet as it traverses through the Linux network stack. The \texttt{XDP\_SKB} hook as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:xdp-receive-path} is invoked after such an \texttt{sk\_buff} has been created and thus replaces the \texttt{xdp\_md} struct for an \texttt{sk\_buff}. Created by the \ac{NIC} driver, the \texttt{sk\_buff} encapsulates the raw packet data and associated metadata such as pointers to the different protocol headers, the network interface on which the packet arrived, flags, and the length of the packet. It is used by every networking layer from the driver through to IP routing, firewalling, transport layers, and the userspace application's socket. It is designed to offer efficient in-kernel manipulation for operations like adding and stripping headers, and splitting and merging packets.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={The sk\_buff struct}, label={lst:sk-buff}]
type block struct {
	struct sk_buff {
        union {unnamed_union};
        __u16 inner_transport_header;
        __u16 inner_network_header;
        __u16 inner_mac_header;
        __be16 protocol;
        __u16 transport_header;
        __u16 network_header;
        __u16 mac_header;
        sk_buff_data_t tail;
        sk_buff_data_t end;
        unsigned char * head;
        unsigned char * data;
        unsigned int truesize;
        atomic_t users;
    };  
}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{XDP Actions}
\label{subsec:xdp-actions}
Once an \ac{eBPF} program has finished running within the XDP hook, it must return one of a few predefined actions, which then instruct the kernel on how to proceed. Table~\ref{tab:xdp_actions} shows the six different possible return codes. For instance, if a program's sole purpose is to gather statistics on packets traversing a specific queue and \ac{NIC}, \texttt{XDP\_PASS} offers a non-intrusive approach, allowing the packet to continue its journey up the network stack as usual. Alternatively, if a program aims to discard irrelevant packets, perhaps based on a packet's source IP within a \ac{DDoS} mitigation tool, \texttt{XDP\_DROP} represents one of the earliest and cheapest ways to reduce the load on the system from malicious packets. \texttt{XDP\_ABORTED} functions similarly to \texttt{XDP\_DROP} but with the additional caveat that an error has occurred. For scenarios like load balancing that distribute traffic across multiple machines, \texttt{XDP\_TX} is highly effective in that it sends the packet back out the same interface it arrived at. Finally, \texttt{XDP\_REDIRECT} can be used for layer 3 routing or in a special case, sending the raw layer 2 frame directly to userspace, thereby skipping the network stack entirely.

        \texttt{XDP\_ABORTED} & Indicates an error or that an exception occurred within the XDP program, leading to the packet being dropped. This is similar to \texttt{XDP\_DROP} but signals a problem. \\

\subsection{AF\_XDP}
\label{sec:af-xdp}
Table~\ref{tab:xdp_actions} highlights the versatility of \texttt{XDP\_REDIRECT}. Beyond directing packets to other interfaces, it offers a powerful mechanism to send them directly to userspace. This capability is particularly beneficial for performance-sensitive protocols such as QUIC, because it completely bypasses the traditional kernel network stack, thereby eliminating its associated overhead. Achieving this direct delivery requires a userspace application to utilize an \texttt{AF\_XDP} socket.

\subsubsection{Setting up an AF\_XDP Socket}
\label{subsec:af-xdp-socket}
After an application establishes its \texttt{AF\_XDP} socket through the socket(2) system call, the next step is to set aside a special memory area for incoming packets. This area is called the UMEM region. When creating UMEM, two primary considerations come into play.

First, there's deciding on the size of each "chunk" within this region. Since each chunk holds one packet, it must be large enough for the biggest packet an application anticipates, but not so large that it wastes significant memory. The Linux kernel requires this chunk size to be a power of two, falling between 2048 bytes and the system's page size (which is often 4096 bytes, making 2048 or 4096 byte chunks common).

Second, the total number of chunks must be determined. If this number is too small, there is a risk of the UMEM region filling up and packets being dropped. However, making it too large simply consumes excess memory. Unfortunately, there is no magic formula here; the ideal number of chunks is entirely specific to an application's unique needs.

Once this entire setup is complete, the result is effectively a large, contiguous block of memory that functions very much like an array, ready to receive packets.

Once the UMEM region is configured, the application establishes a communication channel with the kernel (specifically, the \ac{eBPF} program) using fixed-size, pre-allocated circular ring buffers. This design choice enables highly efficient lockless operations in a single-producer, single-consumer model, as it avoids further memory allocation. However, a direct consequence of this fixed capacity is that when a ring becomes full, new packets are dropped. To therefore ensure data integrity and avoid packet loss, the consumer's processing speed must consistently surpass the producer's rate.

At this point, the userspace process contains ownership over all the chunks. This is represented in Figure~\ref{fig:umem-after-initialization}.

The userspace application then yields ownership of a specified number of UMEM chunks to the kernel, preparing them to receive incoming packets. This is achieved by generating descriptors, each uniquely pointing to a UMEM chunk, and enqueuing them into the \texttt{Fill queue} (as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:umem-owned-by-fill-queue}). Subsequently, the kernel can then retrieve these descriptors and use the associated UMEM chunks to store packets as they arrive from the NIC (this process is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:umem-owned-by-kernel}).

Once the packets have been copied into the UMEM chunks controlled by the kernel, it moves the descriptors to the \texttt{RX queue} as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:add-descs-to-fill-queue}. The process may then consume these packets.

Once the application has consumed a descriptor from the RX queue, it updates the \texttt{RX queue}'s tail pointer and gains two options for handling the associated frame. First, it can return the descriptor to the \texttt{Fill queue}, effectively relinquishing control of the frame back to the kernel for future incoming packets. Alternatively, the application can populate the frame with its own data and add the descriptor to the \texttt{TX queue}. In this scenario, control passes to the kernel with the explicit intent of transmitting the packet out onto the network interface (as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:add-desc-to-tx-queue}). Upon successful transmission, the kernel consumes the TX descriptor and signals completion by adding a new descriptor to the \texttt{Completion queue}.

Throughout the lifetime of the process, this interplay of constantly consuming and producing descriptors on the ring buffers enables the transmission and reception of packets with minimal locking and low system call overhead due to the nature of batch sending and receiving the descriptors. Given that packet I/O is the greatest limiting factor to QUIC performance, AF\_XDP therefore offers an attractive value proposition with its cheap packet delivery.

\subsubsection{XDP Options}
\label{subsec:xdp-options}
Despite AF\_XDP being a relatively new technology, there are a number of available features to change and enhance the way it functions.

\subsubsection{Zero-Copy}
\label{subsec:zero-copy}
By default, when a packet arrives at the \ac{NIC}, the traditional kernel receive path involves copying the incoming data into a buffer within the RX ring, as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:xdp-receive-path}. When AF\_XDP is active without specific optimizations, the network driver then has to perform a second copy, moving this packet data from the kernel's buffer into a UMEM frame. This double-copy operation introduces noticeable overhead, consuming both CPU cycles and memory bandwidth.

However, this costly double-copy can be entirely bypassed by enabling zero-copy mode. This is achieved by passing the \texttt{XDP\_ZEROCOPY} flag to the \texttt{bind(2)} syscall when an \ac{XSK} binds with a queue on the NIC. With zero-copy enabled, the \ac{NIC} is instructed to directly place the incoming Layer 2 packet into a UMEM frame. This means the packet data never needs to be copied into an intermediate kernel buffer, resulting in what should be significantly reduced latency, lower CPU utilization, and higher packet throughput. It is important to note however, that the success of zero-copy depends on the network driver's support and the NIC's capabilities, as it requires direct interaction between the hardware and the UMEM region.

\subsubsection{XDP Hardware Offload}
\label{subsec:xdp-hardware-offload}
In the effort to minimize load on the CPU, it has now also become possible to offload an XDP program to the \ac{NIC}. Pioneered by Netronome, their SmartNIC is able to directly ingest the \ac{eBPF} bytecode of an \ac{XDP} program, compile it to the native instruction set, and execute the logic before the packet reaches the host CPU's memory as shown in Kicinski et. al~\cite{Kicinski2017XDP}. The implication of such a change is a reduction in latency and an increase in throughput. This is largely due to eliminating the need for costly \ac{DMA} transfers into kernel memory and avoiding the traditional hardware interrupt path.

\section{Precision Time Protocol}
\label{sec:ptp}
Throughout history, humans have continuously refined methods of timekeeping to enable synchronization among multiple entities. From sundials to pendulum clocks, the need for precise time has spurred ever-finer-grained models. \ac{PTP}, defined by the \ac{IEEE} as \ac{IEEE}-1588, is a protocol to synchronize clocks in a computer network~\cite{IEEE1588_2019_PTP} and represents the latest step in this evolution. Several versions exist, each of which supersedes the previous version: PTPv1 (\ac{IEEE} 1588-2002), PTPv2 (\ac{IEEE} 1588-2008), and PTPv2.1 (\ac{IEEE} 1588-2019). \ac{PTP} is accurate to the sub-microsecond, can function over Ethernet or IP, and is already widely deployed in industry ~\cite{MetaPTP2022}.

Setting up a \ac{PTP} network involves a clear sequence of steps. Initially, each node within the network must be configured. Thereafter, the Best Master Clock Algorithm, as detailed in Subsection~\ref{subsec:best-master-clock-algorithm}, is executed to determine the optimal master clock. The final step is for all nodes to synchronize their clocks, a process elaborated upon in Subsection~\ref{subsec:synchronization}. It is imperative for a successful synchronization that the nodes align their clock \emph{frequencies}, \emph{phases}, and \emph{times}.

\subsection{PTP Profiles}
\label{subsec:ptp-profiles}
The PTPv2.1 (\ac{IEEE} 1588-2019) standard includes various \emph{profiles} that adapt the core \ac{PTP} protocol to meet the specific requirements of different industries. The \ac{PTP} topology developed for this thesis utilizes the \ac{IEEE} 802.1AS profile~\cite{4659212}, commonly known as \emph{\ac{gPTP}}. It is primarily used in Ethernet local area networks for applications such as audio and video, and falls under the \ac{TSN} umbrella. A key advantage of \ac{gPTP} is its operation directly at Ethernet Layer 2, which reduces overhead compared to other profiles that function at Layer 3.

\subsection{PTP Tools}
\label{subsec:ptp-tools}
Within Linux, three primary tools are used to carry out the clock synchronization in \ac{PTP}: \texttt{ptp4l}, \texttt{phc2sys}, and \texttt{pmc}. These tools are all part of the \emph{linuxptp} project, which is an open-source implementation of the \ac{PTP} specification~\cite{LinuxPTP}. \texttt{ptp4l} is a daemon that synchronizes the \ac{PHC} of the \ac{NIC} to match the time of a \ac{PTP} master clock. It accomplishes this by exchanging \ac{PTP} messages (i.e. Sync, Follow\_Up, Delay\_Req, Delay\_Resp) with the master clock as further described in Subsection~\ref{subsec:synchronization}. These messages allow \texttt{ptp4l} to calculate the offset, delay, and drift between the slave and master \ac{PHC}s, all of which are necessary to maintain high accuracy and precision. \texttt{phc2sys} is a daemon that disciplines the \texttt{CLOCK\_REALTIME} (i.e. the system clock) or \ac{PHC}s with a 'master' \ac{PHC}. This is desirable for a few reasons. First, \texttt{phc2sys} allows the system times of two different computers to be synced with each other, which is crucial for scenarios demanding high temporal accuracy like data logging or event correlation. Second, using \texttt{phc2sys} to sync multiple \ac{PHC}s is advantageous when network topology constraints prevent them from being connected through traditional \ac{PTP} means. An example of such a topology can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:ptp-topology}. Finally, \texttt{pmc} is a tool used to configure \texttt{ptp4l} during run-time.

\subsection{Time Types}
\label{subsec:time-types}
As already alluded to, the concept of "time" itself can be defined and represented in various ways, each with distinct characteristics and applications. While numerous time representations exist, \ac{PTP} primarily interacts with a select few, each serving different purposes in achieving precise synchronization. This subsection details these relevant time types, from human-centric civil time to highly stable atomic timescales and the internal clocks found within computer systems.

\subsubsection{Coordinated Universal Time (UTC)}
\label{subsubsec:utc}
\ac{UTC} is a timescale tied to the rotation of the Earth and is used for legal, civil, and daily life. Different time zones can be described as offsets from the \ac{UTC} time. So, for example, Berlin can be represented as \emph{UTC+2}, meaning that it is the current \ac{UTC} time plus two hours. Unfortunately, one downside to \ac{UTC} is that because it is tied to the Earth's rotation, which is not perfectly consistent, it is necessary to introduce some form of adjustment in order to keep it in sync with the solar day. This manifests itself in the form of leap seconds. Logically, leap seconds are problematic for high-precision systems because they introduce discontinuities and make time calculations more complex. Thus, another method is needed.

\subsubsection{International Atomic Time (TAI)}
\label{subsubsec:tai}
\ac{TAI} is an atomic timescale that shares the reference point of January 1st, 1970 with \ac{UTC}; however, it differs in that it is bound to the atomic scale (e.g. measuring the vibrations of atoms like Caesium-133) as opposed to Earth's rotation. This is beneficial as the behavior of certain atoms are much more precise, which results in an extremely robust monotonically increasing clock. Converting \ac{TAI} to \ac{UTC} requires knowing how many leap seconds have occurred since the reference point in 1970. As of July 2025, there have been 37 leap seconds, which means $UTC = TAI - 37$. The benefit is that data centers using synchronized hardware working in \ac{TAI} are still able to store timestamps in \ac{UTC} for clients to consume.

\subsubsection{CLOCK\_REALTIME}
\label{subsubsec:clock-realtime}
Most computers have an internal oscillator on the motherboard they use to keep their best approximation of \ac{UTC} time. The \texttt{CLOCK\_REALTIME} is a Linux-native label used to interact with this time. When \texttt{phc2sys} is used to alter the system time, it references \texttt{CLOCK\_REALTIME}. This clock is therefore susceptible to \ac{PTP}.

\subsubsection{CLOCK\_MONOTONIC}
\label{subsubsec:clock-monotonic}
Beyond approximating \ac{UTC}, the internal oscillator also maintains a predictable clock, accessible as \texttt{CLOCK\_MONOTONIC}. This clock is useful for measuring intervals, with typical use cases including benchmarks, timeouts, and scheduling. It is monotonically increasing, usually commencing at system boot, and is unaffected by \ac{PTP} adjustments.

\subsection{NIC Hardware Clocks}
\label{subsec:nic-hw-clocks}
Besides the oscillator found on the computer's motherboard, some \ac{NIC}s are equipped with their own internal oscillators for timekeeping, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ptp-timestamping-options}. These \ac{PHC}s are crucial for achieving extremely accurate timestamping because they are inherently immune to the scheduling delays that can arise with software timestamps in Linux.

To verify whether a \ac{NIC} supports hardware timestamping, the \texttt{ethtool -T} command can be used. A strong indication of hardware timestamping capability, and therefore suitability for \ac{PTP}, is the presence of \emph{hardware-receive}, \emph{hardware-transmit}, and \emph{PTP Hardware Clock: <num>} in the command's output.

As a word of caution, it is important to note that some \ac{NIC}s may only offer hardware timestamps up to a certain bit rate or packet rate. Therefore, referencing the \ac{NIC} documentation is essential to determine these specific limitations.

\subsection{Clock Types}
\label{subsec:clock-types}
To maintain its high resolution, \ac{PTP} employs a hierarchy of clocks, each of which fulfills a distinct function. Crucially, no non-\ac{PTP}-aware devices should exist between \ac{PTP} nodes, as they can disrupt the extremely delicate synchronization efforts. Furthermore, a tree-topology is always maintained. Redundant ports are automatically switched from 'slave' (S) or 'master' (M) states into a 'passive' (P) state to prevent loops. An example topology is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ptp-network-with-pruning}.

\subsubsection{Ordinary Clock}
\label{subsubsec:ordinary-clock}
The \ac{OC} is a device type in the \ac{PTP} network that has a single \ac{PTP} port and functions either as a source (master) or a destination (slave) with respect to clock synchronization. These devices typically do not participate in the active distribution of time to other network segments. There can be multiple master and slave \ac{PHC}s in the network.

\subsubsection{Grandmaster Clock}
\label{subsubsec:grandmaster-clock}
The \ac{GMC} serves as the primary and typically most accurate time source for the entire \ac{PTP} network. Characterized by its superior clock quality, it often contains direct access to highly precise external references such as a \ac{GPS} receiver or an atomic clock. A \ac{PTP} network can only have one \ac{GMC} at any given time, and every \ac{GMC} inherently assumes the role of a master clock.

\subsubsection{Boundary Clock}
\label{subsubsec:boundary-clock}
The \ac{BC} is a device that acts as both a slave to an upstream master clock, synchronizing its own time, and then as a master to downstream slave clocks, distributing that synchronized time to other network segments. It is typically a multi-port, \ac{PTP}-aware network device, and is often implemented within layer 2 switches. Fundamentally, it exists to scale \ac{PTP} deployments to more complex topologies and reduce the synchronization load on the master.

\subsubsection{Transparent Clock}
\label{subsubsec:transparent-clock}
The \ac{TC} is a network device, typically an Ethernet switch as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:ptp-topology}, whose main purpose is to measure and compensate the time a \ac{PTP} message spends within the device itself. This 'residence time' is calculated by timestamping the moment of the packet's arrival $t_1$. When the \ac{PTP} message leaves the \ac{TC}, the egress timestamp $t_2$ is taken, and the difference between $t_2-t_1$ is taken and added to the \texttt{CorrectionField} within the particular \texttt{Sync} message. The slave may then use this field when calculating its clock's offset and delay. This synchronization process is further described in Subsection~\ref{subsec:synchronization}.

A key advantage of Transparent Clocks is their ability to achieve a flatter \ac{PTP} topology, contrasting with Boundary Clocks which inherently increase the depth of the tree topology. Also, Transparent Clocks are technically not part of the \ac{PTP} master and slave state machine the way the other clock types are; however, when used in a \ac{P2P} setup as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:ptp-tc-sync}, the \ac{TC} will additionally calculate the propagation delays of the master and slave links. It will then add the master link delay to the \texttt{CorrectionField} as well.

\subsection{Best Master Clock Algorithm}
\label{subsec:best-master-clock-algorithm}
The \ac{BMCA} runs on each node within the \ac{PTP} network to establish and maintain a hierarchy of \ac{PHC}s. To initiate this process, master-node candidates transmit "Announce" messages to their neighbors. By default, these messages are sent every second, although this frequency is configurable, subject to hardware manufacturer constraints~\cite{CiscoPTP}. These messages allow the topology to remain dynamic and adapt when changes in the network occur.

Each Announce message carries the properties of the transmitting node's \ac{PHC} as its payload, and as detailed in Table~\ref{tab:bmca-attributes}, each Announce message includes the following attributes: \texttt{priority1}, \texttt{clockClass}, \texttt{clockAccuracy}, \texttt{offsetScaledLogVariance}, \texttt{priority2}, and \texttt{clockIdentity}. When it receives an Announce message, the node checks the advertised \texttt{priority1} against its own, using each subsequent attribute as a potential tie-breaker. If the node determines itself as master, it will continue transmitting Announce messages. Otherwise, it changes into a slave and stops transmitting them.

\subsection{Synchronization}
\label{subsec:synchronization}
Once the \ac{PTP} topology is established, the \ac{PHC}s begin their synchronization process. At a high level, a master \ac{PHC} aims to achieve three key objectives when disciplining its slaves: frequency, phase, and time synchronization. Synchronizing the frequency ensures both clocks tick at the same rate. Synchronizing the phase means the clocks tick at precisely the same instant. Finally, synchronizing the times ensures both clocks agree on the absolute time of day.

Figure~\ref{fig:ptp-frequency-synchronization} depicts the message sequence for a slave to achieve frequency synchronization with its master. The master initiates this by dispatching two \texttt{Sync} messages and recording their egress timestamps, $t_{m}^{1} \text{ and } t_{m}^{2}$, at the moment they exit the \ac{NIC}. These timestamps are subsequently transmitted to the slave in individual \texttt{Follow\_Up} messages. In parallel, the slave generates ingress timestamps, $t_{s}^{1} \text{ and } t_{s}^{2}$, upon the \texttt{Sync} messages' arrival at its \ac{NIC}'s port. Now equipped with these four timestamp values, the slave possesses enough information to compute its clock drift relative to the master.

The calculation proceeds by first determining the time intervals on both the master and slave sides: $\Delta_{m}=t_{m}^{2}-t_{m}^{1}$ and $\Delta_{s}=t_{s}^{2}-t_{s}^{1}$. Thereafter, the slave's relative frequency drift is computed as $\text{Drift}=\frac{\Delta_{s}-\Delta_{m}}{\Delta{s}}$. If this drift value is positive, it signifies the slave's clock is advancing faster than the master's, thus requiring a reduction in speed. Conversely, a negative drift value indicates the slave's clock is lagging, necessitating an increase in speed.

With the frequency synchronized, the slave clock proceeds to synchronize its phase and time-of-day. This is achieved by precisely calculating the propagation delay and the clock offset. The propagation delay represents the time a packet takes to traverse the physical medium from master to slave. The offset is the necessary adjustment to the slave's clock to align it with the master's, after accounting for this propagation delay.

Figure~\ref{fig:ptp-synchronization} illustrates the message exchange for calculating these two values. The master initiates the process by sending a \texttt{Sync} message, capturing its departure timestamp, $t_1$, as it leaves the \ac{NIC}. The master then sends a \texttt{Follow\_Up} message containing the $t_1$ timestamp in its payload. Upon arrival at the slave, the \texttt{Sync} message is timestamped to produce $t_2$. The slave subsequently sends a \texttt{Delay\_Req} message to the master, recording its departure timestamp, $t_3$, from the \ac{NIC}. When the \texttt{Delay\_Req} arrives at the master, a final timestamp, $t_4$ is generated and promptly included in a \texttt{Delay\_Resp} message sent back to the slave.

Equipped with $t_1, t_2, t_3, \text{ and } t_4$, the slave can now calculate the offset and delay. We define two intermediate values as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:ptp-synchronization}: $A=t_2-t_1=\text{delay}+\text{offset}$ and $B=t_4-t_3=\text{delay}-\text{offset}$. From these, the propagation delay and offset can be derived as follows: $\text{delay}=\frac{A+B}{2}$ and $\text{offset}=\frac{A-B}{2}$. As an example, using the values from Figure~\ref{fig:ptp-synchronization}, if $A=t_2-t_1=58-53=5$ and  $B=t_4-t_3=64-63=1$, then the calculated offset is ${offset}=\frac{A-B}{2}=\frac{5-1}{2}=2$ and the delay is ${delay}=\frac{A+B}{2}=\frac{5+1}{2}=3$. The slave then adjusts its local time by this calculated offset to maintain synchronization with the master.

The synchronization process just described is known as the "two-step setup," due to the separate \texttt{Sync} and \texttt{Follow\_Up} messages. An alternative, "one-step setup," integrates the $t_1$ timestamp directly into the \texttt{Sync} message payload, thereby eliminating the need for a separate \texttt{Follow\_Up} message. By default, \texttt{Sync} and \texttt{Delay\_Req} messages are sent once per second, but their frequency can be adjusted from 8 times per second to once every 2 seconds~\cite{CiscoPTP}.

Finally, it's important to note that Figure~\ref{fig:ptp-synchronization} depicts the Peer-to-Peer model for delay and offset calculation within \ac{PTP}. An alternative, the End-to-End model, also exists but is excluded here for brevity and because this thesis's PTP topology utilizes the Peer-to-Peer model.

\section{MsQuic}
\label{sec:msquic}
MsQuic\footnote{https://github.com/microsoft/msquic}, Microsoft's QUIC implementation, was selected for the measurements in this thesis due to its high-throughput performance, as demonstrated in Kempf et al.~\cite{kempf2024quicperf}. Furthermore, it is one of the few QUIC implementations that supports \ac{XDP}. Developed for Windows, Linux, FreeBSD, and macOS, MsQuic employs a two-layered architecture to achieve cross-platform scalability: the QUIC layer and the \ac{PAL}.

The QUIC layer encapsulates the core QUIC protocol logic and generally remains consistent across different builds. In contrast, the \ac{PAL} provides abstractions for critical components such as \ac{TLS}, \ac{UDP}, and operating system primitives like threads and locks~\cite{MsQuicArchitecture}. While this architecture introduces some complexity, it offers the significant advantage of modularity, enabling "plug-and-play" functionality for various components. For instance, users can choose from three \ac{TLS} variants: OpenSSL1.1\footnote{https://openssl-library.org/news/openssl-1.1.1-notes/index.html}, OpenSSL3\footnote{https://openssl-library.org/news/openssl-3.0-notes/index.html}, and Schannel\footnote{https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/com/schannel}.

This modular design also allows for fine-grained control over the datapath, which is where \ac{XDP} integration becomes relevant. To establish a baseline for comparison with \ac{XDP}, the subsequent subsections will detail the existing datapath enhancements already incorporated into MsQuic.

\subsection{Receiving Data}
\label{subsec:receiving-data}

\begin{figure}
	\centering

Figure~\ref{fig:buffers} shows how a packet flows through the kernel to reach a socket buffer, after which a system call such as \texttt{recvfrom(2)} can pull the packet's data into userspace. Upon arrival at the \ac{NIC}, the packet is directly \ac{DMA}-ed into a buffer within kernel memory, and a descriptor within the RX ring is updated to indicate the buffer contains a valid packet. This is depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:recvfrom}. The \ac{NIC} then signals the \ac{CPU} via an interrupt request, indicating packet arrival. In response, a NAPI\footnote{https://docs.kernel.org/networking/napi.html} context is established, which subsequently polls the \ac{NIC} for further packets.

With the packet now present in the buffer, the \ac{NIC} driver creates an \texttt{sk\_buff} structure, which holds references to the packet data in the RX ring, along with essential metadata. This \texttt{sk\_buff} then traverses the various layers of the network stack, undergoing necessary processing steps, until it is ultimately enqueued into the socket's receive buffer. Finally, the \texttt{recvfrom(2)} system call is invoked, triggering a context switch that copies the packet data from the kernel buffer into the userspace application's memory.

It accomplishes this by providing the kernel with an array of buffers and instructs it to fill as many as possible with available packet data. Fundamentally, this enables the application to \emph{batch} several receive operations into a single system call, which amortizes the cost of the context switch.

\subsubsection{Generic Receive Offload}
\label{subsubsec:gro}
One downside to the approaches above is that an \texttt{sk\_buff} is allocated for every MTU-sized packet that arrives on the \ac{NIC}. Each \texttt{sk\_buff} then individually goes through each of the network stack processing steps, even if the packets are part of the same flow. To improve on this design, \ac{GRO} can be used, which assembles packets as part of the same flow under the umbrella of a single \texttt{sk\_buff}. Given that the currently maximum allowed \ac{GRO} packet size is 64k bytes\footnote{https://elixir.bootlin.com/linux/v6.15.4/source/include/linux/netdevice.h\#L2319} and a common MTU size is 1.5k bytes, this can lead to a reduction in processing by a factor of $\frac{64}{1.5}=~43$. As discussed Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, this improves performance significantly. A representation of this \texttt{sk\_buff} can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:gro}.

\subsection{Logging}
\label{subsec:logging}
MsQuic generates logs and traces using CLOG~\cite{MsQuicDiagnostics}, a cross-platform diagnostic tool developed by Microsoft. On Linux, CLOG seamlessly integrates with LTTng\footnote{https://lttng.org/}, allowing for the production of logs with minimal overhead. Beyond its efficiency, CLOG's messages are highly structured, making them easy to parse for subsequent post-processing. This robust logging capability, as further detailed in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, plays an extensive role in the \ac{PTP} measurements.

\section{pos Measurement Framework}
\label{sec:pos-measurement-framework} 
Measurements for this thesis utilized a modified \ac{QIR} framework, as described by Jaeger et al.~\cite{10186365}. This setup exclusively uses bare metal machines, eliminating potential noise introduced by virtualization software. The network topology for these measurements is detailed in Figure~\ref{fig:qir-topology}.

For each measurement, a file-download scenario between a client and server was executed, allowing for throughput inference. A dedicated management node was responsible for resetting machines to a baseline state (e.g., Debian Bookworm) and distributing necessary files and environment variables. Splitters were incorporated as non-invasive means to duplicate packets exchanged between the client and server, enabling the sniffer to capture them, which was crucial for \ac{PTP} tests.

The core advantage of the \ac{QIR} framework lies in its emphasis on repeatability. It allows for the declarative specification of runtime environments, QUIC implementations, test types, and pre- and post-processing steps, all handled automatically by the \ac{QIR}.
